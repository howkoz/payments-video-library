The Network Transaction ID and the network token

A guide for Customer Service, Finance and anyone outside Payments. Two numbers travel with a stored card. They do different jobs. Facts as recorded to 25 November 2026.

The digits in the pictures are made up.

Network token

A token is a stand-in card number. Visa or Mastercard issues it for one card and one company. The company uses the token in place of the real card number.

Network Transaction ID

The NTI is the receipt number of the first payment of a subscription. Every later rebill quotes it. The bank then knows the customer agreed to the series.

Part 1. How the network token works

How a token is made

  1. A customer saves a card with us.
  2. We ask Visa or Mastercard for a token for that card.
  3. The network checks the card with the customer's bank.
  4. The network sends us the token. We store it beside the card.

We ask under a Token Requestor ID. This is our company's name tag at Visa and Mastercard. The token belongs to whoever owns that ID.

What travels on a payment

WhenWhat we send
The customer is paying nowThe token and a one-time code from the network (a cryptogram)
A rebill, with no customer presentThe token and the NTI of the first payment
The token does not workThe real card number, as a fallback

Why it helps

  • The bank replaces a lost or expired card. The network keeps the token working, so the subscription keeps paying.
  • The real card number does not travel on the payment.
  • A token that Unicity owns can go to any processor that accepts it.
Watch out. Some processors offer their own tokens, for example Worldpay's Revenue Boost. Those tokens sit under the processor's Token Requestor ID, not ours. They help approvals at that processor. They cannot move with us to another processor.
Where Unicity stands. Unicity does not have its own Token Requestor ID yet. The plan, set on 15 October 2026, is to ask Visa and Mastercard directly under our own ID. A new vault alone does not give us tokens: we must enrol with the card networks first.

Part 2. How the Network Transaction ID works

Three rules about the NTI

  • Only the processor can give it to us. It comes back in the reply to the first payment. Nobody requests it.
  • It belongs to the subscription, not to the card. Two subscriptions on one card each keep their own NTI.
  • A new card or a new subscription starts a new series with its own first payment.
Where Unicity stands. Of 96,653 active card subscriptions, 11,255 hold a real NTI: about 12 in 100. In Europe, where rebills carry these fields, approval rose from 56.8% (783 of 1,378) to 67.1% (760 of 1,133), 14 days each side. Card subscriptions in the United States and Canada rebilled with no NTI at the 9 November 2026 count.

Part 3. How they work together on a rebill

A token without the NTI is a known card with no proof of the agreement. An NTI without a token still works, but it can break when the bank replaces the card.

Part 4. Quick answers

Are the token and the NTI the same thing?

No. The token stands in for the card. The NTI is the receipt number of the first payment of one subscription.

A customer got a new card. Does the subscription stop?

With a network token, the network keeps the token working when the bank replaces the card. Unicity does not have its own tokens yet.

Why does a rebill decline as a "stranger"?

The rebill carries no NTI. The bank cannot link it to the payment the customer approved.

Can we ask a processor for a customer's NTI?

No. The processor returns it in the reply to the first payment. If we did not keep it then, the next first payment makes a new one.

A processor says "we give you network tokens". Is that enough?

Ask whose Token Requestor ID they sit under. If it is the processor's ID, the tokens cannot move with us.
